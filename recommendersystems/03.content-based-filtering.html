Part 2: The Deep Learning Architecture (Two Towers)

This is the state-of-the-art design used in many commercial systems today.
In industry it's often called a two-tower architecture.

The Two Networks

User network: takes (age, gender, country, ...) → a few dense layers → outputs , a vector of 32 numbers.

item network: takes (year, stars, ...) → a few dense layers → outputs , also 32 numbers.

Key architectural facts:

  • The final layer is not a single unit (unlike typical regression/classification networks) it's a 32-unit layer outputting the embedding vector.
  • The two networks can have different architectures (different depths, widths) only the output dimension must match (32 = 32).
  • The final layer has no activation (linear) should be free to take any real values.

Part 3: Scaling Up Retrieval and Ranking

The Problem

Catalogs can be thousands of movies, millions of ads or products, or tens of millions of songs. Running the neural network on every (user, item) pair each time a user loads the page is computationally infeasible.

Solution: a two-step pipeline.

Step 1: Retrieval Cheap and Broad

Generate a large list of plausible candidates using fast, non-neural-network methods. It's explicitly OK to include items the user won't like the goal is broad coverage, ensuring good items are somewhere in the list.

Then Enhance them: merge the lists, remove duplicates, and remove items the user has already watched/purchased (you usually don't re-recommend those).

Result: ~100 to a few hundred candidates, retrieved in essentially O(1) time via lookups.

Step 2: Ranking Slow but Accurate

Now run the real model on the small candidate set:

  • For each candidate item, compute the predicted rating (or with the sigmoid variant).
  • Sort and display the top-ranked items.

    The big optimization: precompute for all items in advance. Then per user visit you only need:
  1. One forward pass through the user network → (a single inference call)
  2. Dot products of with the ~hundreds of candidate 's

A dot product in 32 dimensions is ~32 multiply-adds trivially cheap. Compare:

Approach Work per user visit
Naive ~10,000,000 full network inferences
Retrieval + ranking A few table lookups + 1 user-network inference + ~500 cheap dot products

This is how production systems deliver results that are both fast and accurate: retrieval prunes the catalog brutally but safely (cheap methods, tolerant of false positives), and ranking spends the expensive, accurate model only where it counts.

Tuning the Retrieval Size

How many items should retrieval pass to ranking 100? 500? 1,000?

  • More retrieved items → better final recommendations (ranking has more to work with), but slower system.
  • How to decide: run offline experiments. If retrieving 500 instead of 100 items substantially raises the model's predicted ratings / estimated of the final recommendations, retrieve more, even at some speed cost.

Part 4: Ethics Recommender Systems Can Amplify Good or Harm

Recommender systems are extremely profitable, but the objective you optimize matters enormously. Look at the range of possible goals:

Objective Assessment
items most likely to be rated 5 stars Fine genuinely serving the user
Products the user is most likely to purchase Fine
Ads with highest Profit-maximizing; can amplify harmful businesses (see below)
Products generating the largest profit for the company (not the most relevant/useful) Legal, but a transparency problem users assume they're seeing what's best for them
Content maximizing watch time / engagement Can amplify harmful content (see below)

Advertising: A Feedback Loop That Amplifies Whatever Wins

Ad revenue ≈ (probability of click) × (advertiser's bid). Whoever bids highest gets the traffic. Two opposite scenarios:

Virtuous cycle good travel company: serving customers well → profitable → can bid more for ads → gets more traffic → more profit → serves even more customers well. The ad system amplifies the best businesses.

Vicious cycle payday loans: the way to win in payday lending (extremely high interest rates charged largely to low-income people) is to be maximally efficient at squeezing every dollar out of customers. The most exploitative company is the most profitable → bids the most for ads → gets the most traffic → exploits more people → bids even more. The ad system amplifies the most exploitative player the opposite of what society wants.

There's no easy fix. One amelioration: refuse ads from exploitative businesses but defining "exploitative" is genuinely hard. These are questions worth open debate.

Engagement Maximization: The Content Problem

It's widely reported that optimizing for watch time / time-on-site has led major social media and video platforms to amplify conspiracy theories and hate/toxic content because such content is highly engaging, even though it harms individuals and society.

Partial amelioration: filter out hate speech, fraud, scams, certain violent content. Again, the definitions are surprisingly tricky an ongoing struggle for companies, individuals, and governments.

Transparency

Many users assume an app recommends what they will enjoy, when it's often optimizing for profit. Recommendation: be transparent with users about what criteria drive your rankings hard to do, but it builds trust and pushes systems toward doing more good.

Bottom line: if you build such a system or any ML system think through not just the benefits but the possible harms, invite diverse perspectives and debate, and build things that make people better off.

Part 5: TensorFlow Implementation (Practice Lab Walkthrough)

Unlike collaborative filtering, this architecture is a standard Keras model (inputs → layers → output), so the usual compile/fit recipe applies no custom training loop needed.

The Two Towers as Sequential Models

# User network
user_net = tf.keras.models.Sequential([
    tf.keras.layers.Dense(256, activation='relu'),   # hidden layer 1
    tf.keras.layers.Dense(128, activation='relu'),   # hidden layer 2
    tf.keras.layers.Dense(32)                        # final layer: 32 units, LINEAR (no activation)
])

# Item (item) network same structure
item_net = tf.keras.models.Sequential([
    tf.keras.layers.Dense(256, activation='relu'),
    tf.keras.layers.Dense(128, activation='relu'),
    tf.keras.layers.Dense(32)
])
  • Hidden layers use the default choice, ReLU.
  • The final 32-unit layer has no activation must be free to take any real values. (Exact hidden-unit counts are in the lab.)

Feeding Inputs, Normalizing, and Joining

# --- User tower ---
vu = user_net(user_features)                    # x_u → v_u  (32 numbers)
vu = tf.linalg.l2_normalize(vu, axis=1)         # normalize v_u to LENGTH 1

# --- Item tower ---
vm = item_net(item_features)                    # x_m → v_m  (32 numbers)
vm = tf.linalg.l2_normalize(vm, axis=1)         # normalize v_m to LENGTH 1

# --- Join: dot product layer ---
output = tf.keras.layers.Dot(axes=1)([vu, vm])  # v_u · v_m  = prediction

model = tf.keras.Model([user_features, item_features], output)

Three special pieces explained:

1. tf.linalg.l2_normalize unit-length normalization. The L2 norm (length) of a vector is

After normalizing both vectors, the dot product becomes pure cosine similarity:

where is the angle between the vectors. Why this helps: the prediction now depends only on direction alignment between user and item representations, not on vector magnitudes. Without it, the network can lower the loss by simply inflating vector norms (a scale game rather than learning genuine match structure). Normalization forces the model to encode match quality in the direction, which stabilizes training and makes predictions comparable across users and items. (Since outputs then live in , in practice you rescale predictions or targets to match your rating scale.)

2. tf.keras.layers.Dot a layer that just takes a dot product. Keras has a dedicated layer type for this (Dot, alongside the familiar Dense). axes=1 means ordinary inner product over the feature dimension. This single layer implements the entire "matching" operation of the architecture.

3. tf.keras.Model(...) declaring inputs and outputs. The overall model takes two inputs (user features, item features) and one output (the dot product). This is a multi-input model another example of composing networks, impossible to express as a single Sequential stack.

Training

The cost function is mean squared error:

model.compile(
    optimizer=tf.keras.optimizers.Adam(learning_rate=...),
    loss=tf.keras.losses.MeanSquaredError()
)
model.fit([user_train, item_train], y_train, epochs=...)

This corresponds exactly to the cost from Part 2 one MSE loss trains all parameters of both towers simultaneously, since gradients flow from the dot product back through both networks.

Final Dense(32) with linear activation Outputs the embedding vector

Core takeaways for application:

  1. Content-based filtering = matching in a learned space. Engineer features for users and items, learn 32-dim embeddings with two neural networks, predict with a dot product.
  2. One MSE cost trains both networks no separate procedures; gradients flow through the dot product into both towers.
  3. L2-normalize both vectors before the dot product it turns the prediction into cosine similarity and makes training behave much better.
  4. Never run the full model over a huge catalog per page view. Retrieve ~100s of candidates with cheap lookups (precomputed similar items, top genres, top in country), then rank with one inference + cheap dot products. Tune retrieval size with offline experiments.
  5. Your training objective is an ethical choice. Optimizing clicks×bids, profit, or engagement can build feedback loops that amplify the best businesses (travel) or the worst (payday loans), and engaging content or toxic content alike. Prefer user-serving objectives and be transparent about your ranking criteria.
  6. Compared to collaborative filtering, this approach solves the cold-start and side-information limitations which is exactly why it's the state of the art in commercial systems today.
